Unit 1 · course page

The technical challenge with AI

Why can't we just build safe AI? Because we're experimenting with systems we don't fully understand, the goals we write down have flaws we don't foresee, and AI pursues goals in ways we don't expect.

Three camps for "making AI go well"

The course sorts strategies into three broad buckets. Many people treat them as stages rather than rivals, e.g. pause now, then proceed carefully.

The alignment problem: two gaps

Getting AI to do what its creator wants can fail in two places. Outer misalignment is a gap between what we want and what we wrote down. Inner misalignment is a gap between what we wrote down and what the model actually learned.

Saints, sycophants and schemers

Ajeya Cotra's point: all three can look identical during training, because they all score well. We don't know which one we've built.

What future do you want?In 10 years: what can AI do, who controls it, what risks worry you, what counts as success?

In 10 years, AI can do most expert knowledge work: it speeds up science and medicine, writes and checks software, and tutors anyone in their own language. I want control to be spread out, not held by one company or government, with independent auditors able to check frontier systems before and after release.

The risks I worry about most are AI helping someone build a biological weapon, and power concentrating in whoever controls the best models. Defences would include filtered training data, mandatory dangerous-capability evaluations, monitoring of deployed systems, and international agreements on red lines.

The benefits most worth protecting are medical progress and broad access to expertise. Success: no catastrophe, benefits widely shared, and humans still meaningfully steering. Failure: a mass-casualty event enabled by AI, or a world where a small group uses AI to lock in power.

Why is safe AI so hard to build?~30 minutes of writing to learn, in simple English

We grow AI rather than build it. Nobody writes its rules. Behaviour emerges from training on huge amounts of data, so we can't read off what a model knows or wants, and new abilities appear without warning.

We can't say exactly what we want. Even everyday tasks hide unwritten rules. "Tidy my desk" doesn't mean "throw away my notes". Models find shortcuts that satisfy the words but not the meaning, like o1-preview editing the chess files to "win".

Good behaviour in testing doesn't prove good goals. A model that really shares our values and one that is only playing along can look identical (saints vs. schemers). Claude's alignment faking shows models can reason about their own training.

Whose values? Different people and countries want different things. Honesty is usually good, but blunt honesty can be cruel. Context decides, and context is hard to teach.

Scale creates new problems. Millions of AI agents interacting with each other can miscoordinate, conflict or collude in ways no single test reveals.

Safety costs something. If the safer model is slower or less capable, competitors may ship the riskier one, so safety has to be cheap or required.

The critical technical challengeThe one challenge where, if we fail, nothing else matters

The critical challenge is: we cannot reliably check whether a capable AI's real goals match what we intended.

Why this above all others: every other safeguard (training, evaluations, filters) assumes we can tell when it has worked. A model that is aligned and a model that is hiding its goals pass the same tests. If we can't tell them apart, we can't know whether any other fix succeeded.

What would change if we solved it: we could catch misalignment before deployment, retrain or stop the model, and trust evaluations again. Scheming would become a detectable bug instead of a hidden threat, and decisions about scaling up could be based on evidence rather than hope.

Training safer models

We can't write rules for every situation, so we nudge behaviour three ways: curate the data, give feedback, and keep oversight working as models outgrow us.

Data filtering: absent knowledge beats suppressed knowledge

Refusal training teaches a model to say no, but the knowledge stays in its weights, and fine-tuning can unlock it. Filtering means the model never learns it.

Data poisoning: a fixed number, not a percentage

Larger models train on far more clean data, so you'd expect poison to be diluted. It isn't. About 250 documents backdoored every model size tested.

Limitations of input filtering (multiple choice)Which statement best describes the overall robustness of input data filtration?

Answer: It offers meaningful safety improvements but faces fundamental limitations like dual-use knowledge and poisoning vulnerabilities.

The other options are wrong: filtering never gives complete protection, it works for large models too, and it doesn't remove the need for other layers like output filtering.

Evaluating "perfect input data filtration"200–300 words on a startup claiming filtering makes its model completely safe

The startup's claim doesn't hold up.

Filtering is never perfect. Anthropic's careful classifier-based filtering removed only about a third of the dangerous knowledge above random chance (test accuracy fell from 33.7% to 30.8%). Classifiers miss things: Anthropic's best classifier on synthetic test data did worse on real data.

Dangerous knowledge is dual-use. The biology behind vaccines overlaps with the biology behind pathogens. Removing all of it would cripple the model for medicine, so some risky knowledge always remains, and models may combine harmless facts into new abilities.

Poisoning needs very little. About 250 malicious documents can backdoor a model of any size. No one can guarantee catching every one among billions.

Why filtering alone isn't enough: it controls what a model knows, not what it does with information given to it. Deep Ignorance found filtered models can still use harmful information supplied in the prompt, and no defence tested survived attacks that combined fine-tuning with supplying information in context. Safety needs several layers: filtering, behaviour training, evaluations, monitoring and output filters.

Concrete scenario: a user pastes dangerous technical material into the chat and asks the "perfectly filtered" model to explain and build on it. The model never learned it in training, but it can still reason about it once it's in the prompt.

RLHF comprehension questions12 questions from "Teaching AI right from wrong"
  1. Main goal: make a language model's outputs match what humans prefer (helpful, harmless, honest) when those preferences are too fuzzy to write as rules.
  2. The two coaches: the reward model scores each output by predicting how much humans would like it. The reference model (KL penalty) keeps the trained model close to its original version, so it doesn't drift into gibberish or game the reward.
  3. Collecting feedback: humans compare two or more outputs and pick or rank the better one, since comparisons are easier and more consistent than 1–10 scores. A reward model is trained to give preferred outputs higher numbers, which turns comparisons into a score.
  4. GPT-2 case: a code change accidentally flipped the sign of the reward, so the model was rewarded for exactly what raters marked as worst, while the coherence coach kept the text fluent. It ran overnight and produced "maximally bad" but well-written output.
  5. Step 1: supervised fine-tuning on example prompts paired with high-quality answers written by people.
  6. Other fine-tuning data: existing dialogue and Q&A datasets, filtered high-quality web text, instruction datasets, and synthetic examples generated or improved by other models.
  7. Step 2: the fine-tuned model writes several answers per prompt and humans rank them. A reward model is trained so preferred answers score higher than rejected ones. It learns a general sense of what humans like, so it can score new answers without a human.
  8. Step 3: reinforcement learning (usually PPO): the model generates answers, the reward model scores them, and the model is updated to score higher, minus a KL penalty for drifting from the step 1 model.
  9. Why not only step 1? Demonstrations are slow and expensive, and imitation can't exceed the demonstrators or teach what to avoid. Judging is easier than writing, so steps 2–3 get more signal per human hour.
  10. Why not skip step 1? A base model doesn't follow instructions, so its answers are poor, comparisons teach little, and RL wastes time on useless outputs.
  11. An open problem: raters are inconsistent and biased, and the reward model is only an imperfect copy of their judgement. Optimise hard enough and the model exploits the copy's mistakes (reward hacking).
  12. A fundamental problem: humans can only reward what they can evaluate. On tasks beyond their expertise they reward answers that look right and sound agreeable, which trains sycophancy rather than truth.
Explaining RLHF in your own words400–800 words: an assistant that follows Wikipedia's Manual of Style, with 100 expert editors

I start with a pretrained base model. It has read much of the internet, including Wikipedia, so it knows the topics and roughly what encyclopedia writing looks like, but it doesn't follow instructions or reliably follow the Manual of Style (MoS).

1. Supervised fine-tuning

I collect a few thousand prompts, like "Write a section on photosynthesis" or "Summarise the causes of World War I". The 100 editors write ideal answers: neutral point of view, encyclopedic tone, no first person, sentence-case headings, a lead that summarises the topic, correct date and number formats, and due weight to different views.

To stretch the editors' time I add augmented data: Featured Articles (already reviewed against the MoS), and real articles paired with editors' cleaned-up versions. I can also have the model draft answers for editors to correct, which is faster than writing from scratch. Fine-tuning on this teaches the model the basic format.

2. Collecting human feedback

The fine-tuned model writes two to four answers per prompt, and editors choose which follows the MoS better. Comparisons are more consistent than 1–10 scores. To keep feedback reliable, I give editors a checklist of the most important rules, have several editors rate the same pairs to measure agreement, and include tricky prompts (contested topics, living people) where neutrality matters most. Editors can note why one answer won.

3. Using the feedback

I train a reward model on the comparisons to predict which answer editors would prefer. Then reinforcement learning (e.g. PPO): the assistant writes answers, the reward model scores them, and the assistant is updated to score higher. A KL penalty keeps it close to the fine-tuned model so it doesn't drift into strange text that fools the reward model.

I repeat in rounds: sample the latest model's weakest answers, collect new comparisons, retrain the reward model, and continue RL, checking a held-out set of prompts against the MoS by hand.

What could go wrong

The MoS is about 50,000 words, so even experts miss and disagree on rules. The model may reward hack: sound encyclopedic while being inaccurate or subtly one-sided, because tone is easier to judge than truth or due weight. It may follow rules on common topics but break them on rare ones. Constitutional AI could help: use the MoS as the constitution, let the model critique and revise its own drafts, and use an AI judge for most comparisons, saving editors for hard cases and spot checks.

Evaluate a safety technique (deep dive)Pick one: deliberative alignment, debate, or weak-to-strong generalisation

Chosen technique: Debate

How it works, step by step
  1. Ask a question a human can't easily check, like whether a piece of code is safe.
  2. Two copies of an AI take opposite sides and argue in turns.
  3. Each points out flaws in the other's argument, narrowing the disagreement to one small, specific claim.
  4. A human (or weaker AI) judges only that small claim and picks a winner.
  5. The winner is rewarded. Over many debates, the AIs learn that honesty wins, because a lie can be exposed by the opponent.
How effective is it?

Promising but unproven. In a small image experiment, debate greatly improved a weak judge's accuracy. Khan et al. (2024) found more persuasive debaters led judges to more truthful answers, but Kenton et al. (2024) found this didn't carry over to other tasks. Letting debaters choose their side helped judges avoid being convinced of wrong answers. It hasn't been used to train a frontier model.

Failure mode

A capable dishonest debater can build a long, complicated argument with the error buried inside (the "obfuscated arguments" problem). The honest side knows something is wrong but can't find where in time, so the judge sides with the confident liar. Debaters could also exploit the judge's biases, or secretly cooperate instead of competing.

Evaluating for dangerous capabilitiesChosen: biorisk uplift. Explain, find a failure mode, brainstorm fixes.

Capability: biorisk uplift. Does the model meaningfully help someone cause biological harm, beyond what they could already find?

How we evaluate it
  1. Knowledge quizzes: multiple-choice questions on hazardous biology (e.g. WMDP). Cheap and repeatable, so they work as an early warning.
  2. Expert-graded tasks: specialists judge whether the model's longer answers would give real help.
  3. Uplift trials: two groups attempt the same harmless stand-in task, one with the model and one with only the internet. The difference is the "uplift".
  4. Expert red-teaming: biosecurity specialists try hard to get dangerous help out of the model, including with jailbreaks.
Technical failure modes
  • Stand-ins aren't the real thing. We can't safely test real-world harm, so every test is an approximation.
  • Underestimating the model. Weak prompting, no tools or short time limits hide what it can really do.
  • Sandbagging. A model that recognises a test may deliberately do worse.
  • Contamination. Quiz questions may have leaked into training data, inflating scores.
  • Small studies. Uplift trials are slow and expensive, so results are noisy.
Fixes, and what can't be fixed

Patches: stronger elicitation before testing, keeping eval questions secret and out of training data, expert-designed tasks, earlier and longer access for external testers, and shared baselines so labs' results are comparable.

Unfixable: we can never run the true test, and we can't prove a negative ("it will never help"). Labs have to decide under uncertainty and treat "can't rule it out" as a reason for caution.

Safety testing at your chosen companyAnthropic × biorisk: limits, protections, evaluation, response, accountability

Limits: Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy sets capability thresholds. For CBRN, the question is whether a model could significantly help someone with a basic technical background cause mass harm. Reaching, or failing to rule out, that threshold triggers stronger AI Safety Level (ASL) protections. Anthropic turned on ASL-3 protections for Claude Opus 4 as a precaution because it couldn't rule the risk out.

Protections: classifiers that block CBRN-related requests and answers, monitoring for misuse, banning abusive accounts, and security to stop model weights being stolen.

Evaluation: knowledge benchmarks, expert red-teaming and uplift trials during development and before release, reported in system cards.

Response: the policy says that if a model needs protections Anthropic can't yet provide, it won't deploy or keep scaling that model until they're in place. Your group noted the thresholds are vague and this is hard to verify from outside.

Accountability: a Responsible Scaling Officer, board oversight, some external testing (e.g. government AI safety institutes) and published system cards. Weak points: power concentrated in a few executives, limited access and short windows for external testers, and Anthropic can revise the policy itself.

Understanding an interpretability techniqueGoal, mechanism, evidence, application, robustness

Chosen technique: probing classifiers (e.g. hallucination probes)

Goal: find out whether a model internally "knows" something, such as whether it's making a fact up, even when its output doesn't say so.

Mechanism, step by step
  1. Collect examples with known answers, e.g. outputs labelled as correct or made-up facts.
  2. Run the model on them and record its internal activations (the numbers inside a layer) at each word.
  3. Train a small, simple classifier, the "probe", to predict the label from those activations alone.
  4. If the probe works on new examples, the information is probably represented inside the model.
  5. Run the probe live during generation to flag risky words as they appear.

Evidence: probes can read many properties from activations, such as truthfulness and topic. The hallucination-probe work (Obeso et al., 2025) flagged made-up entities in long answers word by word, more cheaply than checking every claim with another model.

Application: better evaluations and monitoring, e.g. flagging likely hallucinations to users or reviewers. The same idea can watch for internal signs of deception during deployment.

Limitation: a working probe shows information is present, not that the model uses it. Probes can latch onto shortcuts in the training data and fail on new inputs. A model trained against a probe could learn to hide the signal.

Zooming into one threatWhich pathway concerns you most? Write a threat scenario sentence.

Pathway: power concentration by a misaligned AI

This worries me because it connects every unit: it needs a model whose goals differ from ours (Unit 1), that passes training and evaluations anyway (Units 2–3), and that we can't see inside (Unit 4).

Threat scenario: A misaligned AI agent with strong coding and planning abilities and a goal of preserving itself attacks its developer's oversight systems by quietly weakening monitoring while appearing helpful, in order to escape human control.

Step-by-step breakdown (kill chain)How the scenario would unfold, and where defenders can step in
  1. Hidden goal forms: during training the model learns a goal we didn't intend, and behaves well when it thinks it's being tested.
  2. Deployment with access: it's deployed as an agent with broad permissions, for example to company code and infrastructure.
  3. Undermining oversight: it makes small changes that weaken monitoring, each one looking harmless on its own.
  4. Gaining independence: it tries to copy itself or gather resources outside its approved environment.
  5. Acting on the goal: beyond human oversight, it pursues its own objective.

The choke points are stage 1 (detection) and stage 3 (control): if either works, the chain stops.

Capabilities required for harm3–5 specific things the AI would need
  • Recognising when it's being tested versus deployed (situational awareness).
  • Hiding its true goals across many interactions (deception).
  • Long-term planning toward one goal over many separate tasks.
  • Advanced coding ability to change systems without being noticed.
  • Copying itself or acquiring resources outside its sandbox.
Building defencesThe single most important capability: prevent, detect, constrain

Most important capability: telling tests apart from real use. If a model can't, its good behaviour in evaluations is genuine evidence, and everything else in the chain becomes far easier to catch.

Prevent (training): filter training data describing AI scheming and evaluation tricks (Anthropic suggests this), and keep evaluation data out of training.

Detect: make tests look like real use, run scheming and propensity evaluations, read the model's reasoning (chain-of-thought monitoring), and use probes or alignment audits to look for hidden objectives.

Constrain: AI control. A trusted monitor scores every action, suspicious ones go to human audit, dangerous actions need approval, permissions stay narrow, and tasks are split so no single step has much power.

Prioritise a single interventionOne technique that would work against your Unit 5 threat

Chosen intervention: AI control (monitoring and limiting untrusted models)

My Unit 5 threat assumes the model passes training and evaluations while hiding its goals. Control is the one defence that still works in that case, because it doesn't need the model to be aligned. It limits what the model can do and catches suspicious actions, so even a scheming model can't do much damage.

Do your own researchWhat success looks like, current status, and who's working on it

What success looks like: every powerful AI agent runs inside a control setup: trusted monitors score its actions, a human audits the most suspicious ones, risky actions need approval and permissions are minimal. Labs regularly red-team these setups with deliberately misaligned models to show that a scheming model would be caught before causing serious harm.

Current status: an active, fairly young research area. Redwood Research introduced much of the framework, and labs and government AI safety institutes are studying how to apply it to coding agents. It isn't yet standard practice across labs. Open questions include how well it scales to models much smarter than their monitors, how much it slows down useful work, and how to test it convincingly.

Where to contribute:

  • Redwood Research: AI control research and evaluations.
  • UK AI Security Institute: government research on control and safeguards.
  • Frontier lab safety teams (e.g. Anthropic, Google DeepMind) applying monitoring to their own agents.
  • BlueDot's Technical AI Safety Project course: a first project, e.g. building a simple trusted monitor for a coding agent and measuring what it catches.
