Step 1

Text becomes tokens

A model never sees letters. Text is chopped into tokens (common words, or pieces of rarer ones), and each token is swapped for an ID number.

This is a simplified imitation. Real tokenizers (byte-pair encoding) learn their pieces from data. Common words become a single token, often including the space before them; rare words split into chunks. The · marks a leading space.

Why it matters: the model's whole world is a sequence of IDs. Odd tokenization is why models used to struggle to count the letters in "strawberry".

Step 2

Tokens become vectors

Each ID is looked up in a big table and turned into a list of thousands of numbers: an embedding. Training arranges them so tokens with similar meanings land near each other.

Try it: click a word to see its neighbours

Pick a word on the map.

Real embeddings have thousands of dimensions. This map squashes them into two, so treat distances as a sketch.

Directions carry meaning. The step from "man" to "woman" points the same way as "king" to "queen". This is the same idea as a feature in interpretability: a direction that stands for a concept.

Step 3

Attention: words look at each other

On its own, "it" means nothing. Attention lets each token pull in information from the tokens before it. A layer runs many attention heads at once, each looking for a different pattern.

The whole pattern for this head. Rows are the word looking; columns are the words it looks at. The top-right is empty because a word can't see the future.

Context changes the answer. Change "tired" to "wide" and "it" switches from the animal to the street. Attention is how the model brings that in. These heads are hand-built illustrations of patterns researchers have found in real models.

Step 4

Inside one head: query, key, value

Each token makes three vectors. Its query asks "what am I looking for?". Every earlier token's key answers "here's what I contain". Query·key scores go through a softmax to become weights, and the output is a weighted mix of the tokens' values.

Try it: drag the scores for the query "it"

Values are shown as colours so you can see the mix. Real values are vectors of numbers, and the mix is a weighted average of them.

The formula: output = softmax(Q·Kᵀ / √d) · V. That's all attention is: score, normalise, mix.

Step 5

The residual stream builds up meaning

Each token's vector travels up through dozens of layers. Each layer has an attention block (look at other tokens) and an MLP block (process and recall facts). Both add their output to the stream, so information piles up rather than being overwritten.

"I sat by the river bank and …"

A cartoon. Real models don't store tidy English labels. But probes and SAEs find information like this appearing in the stream layer by layer.

Why safety people care: the residual stream is where interpretability tools read and write. Probes read it; steering adds a feature direction to it.

Step 6

Predicting the next token

At the top, the final vector is compared with every token in the vocabulary to give a score (logit) for each. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and one token is picked. Temperature controls how adventurous the pick is.

Try it: "The Eiffel Tower is in …"
Try it: generate one word at a time

This toy only looks at the previous word. A real model looks at the whole context through attention, but the loop is the same: predict, pick, append, repeat.

That's all generation is: one token at a time, each chosen from a probability distribution. Low temperature is near-deterministic; high temperature gets creative, then incoherent.

Step 7

How it learns

Training shows the model real text, asks it to predict each next token, and measures how surprised it was (the loss). Then every weight is nudged a tiny amount in the direction that would have made the right answer more likely. Repeat trillions of times.
